# Hospital Readmission Risk Factors and Prevention Strategies (AHRQ Framework)

## Introduction
This document synthesizes findings from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) regarding 30-day hospital readmissions, identifying core risk factors and evidence-based interventions to prevent avoidable readmissions.

## Definition of 30-Day Readmission
A 30-day readmission is defined as an admission to an acute care hospital within 30 days of a discharge from the same or another acute care hospital. Medicare and other payors penalize hospitals for high rates of readmissions for specific conditions, notably Heart Failure (HF), Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI), and Pneumonia.

## Clinical Risk Factors
Clinical complexity strongly correlates with readmission risk:
1. **High Comorbidity Burden:** Patients with multiple chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes, COPD, heart failure, chronic kidney disease) face compounded risks.
2. **Prior Hospitalizations:** A history of multiple admissions in the past 6 to 12 months is one of the strongest predictors of future readmissions.
3. **Polypharmacy:** Patients taking 5 or more daily medications, particularly high-risk drugs like insulin, anticoagulants, and sedatives, are at increased risk due to adverse drug events or nonadherence.
4. **Length of Stay:** Prolonged index hospital stays often indicate severe illness or nosocomial complications, elevating readmission risk.
5. **Premature Discharge:** Discharging patients before clinical stability (e.g., unresolved fluid overload in heart failure) significantly guarantees readmission.

## Social Determinants of Health (SDOH)
SDOH significantly impact a patient's ability to recover safely at home:
- **Lack of Social Support:** Living alone without a caregiver limits a patient's ability to manage complex regimens, prepare meals, or attend follow-up appointments.
- **Housing Instability:** Homelessness or inadequate housing prevents safe recovery and medication storage.
- **Transportation Barriers:** Inability to access follow-up care or pharmacies.
- **Health Literacy:** Limited understanding of discharge instructions, warning signs, or medication schedules.
- **Financial Toxicity:** Inability to afford prescribed medications or healthy food.

## Vulnerable Populations
- **Elderly (Frail) Patients:** Increased risk for falls, delirium, and infections.
- **Cognitive Impairment:** Patients with dementia or delirium struggle to follow post-discharge instructions.
- **Behavioral Health Issues:** Untreated depression, anxiety, or substance use disorders severely disrupt recovery and adherence.

## Evidence-Based Interventions for Prevention

### 1. Comprehensive Discharge Planning
- Discharge planning should begin on the day of admission.
- Use the "Teach-Back" method to confirm patient understanding of their diagnosis, medications, and warning signs.
- Provide clear, written instructions translated to the patient's primary language at an appropriate reading level.

### 2. Medication Reconciliation
- A pharmacist or clinician must conduct a rigorous medication reconciliation at admission, transfer, and discharge.
- Identify and resolve discrepancies, duplications, and harmful interactions.
- Ensure the patient has physical access to their new medications before leaving the hospital.

### 3. Early Follow-Up Appointments
- High-risk patients should be seen by a primary care provider or specialist within **7 days** of discharge.
- If an in-person visit is not feasible, a telehealth visit or structured phone call by a nurse within 48 to 72 hours is recommended to assess symptoms and medication adherence.

### 4. Transition Care Management (TCM)
- Utilize multidisciplinary teams (social workers, pharmacists, case managers, community health workers) to bridge the gap between hospital and home.
- Arrange for home health services, durable medical equipment (DME), and social services prior to discharge.

### 5. Red Flag Recognition
- Educate patients and caregivers on specific "red flag" symptoms that require immediate attention (e.g., sudden weight gain in heart failure, signs of hypoglycemia in diabetes).
- Provide a direct contact number (e.g., 24/7 nurse triage line) as an alternative to returning to the Emergency Department.

## Disclaimer
These risk factors and interventions are summarized for clinical decision support. Institutions should implement customized transition programs based on their local population needs and resources.
